Eleven buys on one signal: anatomy of a bug

This is the post most trading blogs would never write. My forex bot spent five days buying the same trade over and over, stacking eleven times its intended size, and I am going to walk you through exactly how dumb it looked in the database.

The system had one safety check that mattered: before entering, it asked the broker whether the position already existed. But sometimes the position query itself failed, and the code treated a failed query the same as an empty answer. Failed fetch equals "no position" equals "safe to buy." It bought again on the next 30 minute scan. And again. And again.

The record shows the whole arc. On October 27, AUD_USD was bought twice on one breakout. On November 3 the same position's exit was logged at price 0.0, because a failed price fetch leaked into the fill record instead of stopping it. On November 5, GBP_USD got bought eight times in about four hours. And from November 12 to 16, USD_CAD bought eleven times: 11,000 units against an intended 1,000.

What did I do? Two things, one of them slow. The stacks were manually trimmed back to intended size, the same week they appeared. And the root cause, the failed-fetch bug, was traced and rewritten with fail-safe logic, deployed November 16. The new rule is simple: when the position query fails, do nothing. No answer is never an invitation to trade. And oversized stacks now block new entries outright.

Here is the part I want on the record, because it is the part people get wrong. I did not clean the database. The fills sit there exactly as they happened, duplicates, zero prices, everything, because a track record you edit is not a record. It is marketing. The honest lab leaves the evidence messy and explains it instead.

Two confessions from the same saga. First, the pattern was visible on October 27 and I did not treat it as urgent until it came back twice more. That is a real lesson about how failures escalate politely. Second, throughout all of this the rules themselves never blinked: the first closed stock trade in late October was a textbook whipsaw loss, taken exactly as prescribed, and a Visa position opened in October rode through the entire incident and closed on its own exit signal, a small win, on November 18, two days after the fix.

A failed fetch is not an empty answer. Almost every trading disaster is some version of that sentence.

Why do I trust this project more now? Because the failure happened, it is documented, and the system now contains a scar where the bug used to be. Scars are evidence. Backtests do not have any.
